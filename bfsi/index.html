Listening Before Default: Voice-Based Borrower Distress Detection for Hindi-Hinglish Collection Calls

Competition: Agentic AI in BFSI (Perfios × Techfest IIT Bombay) — Round 1 Abstract
Track: Voice-based borrower distress detection
Team: ClickCraft Services (Team ID TM-FA34F745439) · Competition ID: PAT-72D21D2E9637

1. The problem

India's lenders talk to borrowers on voice calls, in Hindi, Hinglish and regional languages, and the most expensive failures in that conversation are the quiet ones. A borrower sliding toward default rarely says so plainly. The distress shows up earlier, more softly: longer pauses, a voice that flattens or hurries, repeated requests for "two more days", promises that keep shrinking, and a shift from talking about the business to talking about a family emergency. A human agent hears some of this — only on the calls they take, in the languages they speak. The rest is lost. Once a missed payment becomes a pattern, the lender's options narrow to the worst ones: aggressive recovery that destroys the relationship, or a write-off that could have been a restructured loan.

Small-ticket borrowers — shopkeepers, street vendors, micro-enterprises — are served by thin teams handling thousands of calls. Existing collection software scores a borrower after the payment is missed, from ledger data alone. It never listens to the conversation itself, even though the conversation holds the earliest distress signal. And the voice tools that exist are built for English call centres, not for a kirana owner explaining in Hinglish why this week's settlement is short.

2. Our proposal

We propose a voice-first distress-detection agent that listens to collection calls, in Hindi and Hinglish, and flags early distress before the first missed payment. The agent does not replace the human collector. It sits beside them, transcribing and analysing each call, scoring distress from what the borrower says and how they say it, and handing the collector a short brief — distress level, the evidence behind it, and the recommended next step: a gentle reminder, a restructured repayment offer, or an urgent human callback. It is a co-pilot, not a replacement voice on the line.

The system builds on the voice-first agent core we have built for VyaparAI, our AI operations agent for neighbourhood kirana and pharmacy shops. That core is designed around Hindi and Hinglish voice input over a WhatsApp-style interface, and around matching UPI settlements against bills and khata records. Here we reframe it from the merchant's side of the counter to the lender's side.

3. How it works

First, the call is captured with consent and transcribed by a speech model tuned for Hindi-Hinglish code-switching, including the numbers, dates and UPI references borrowers actually use. Second, transcript and audio are analysed on three axes: content signals (job loss, illness, stock loss, another lender), commitment signals (promises made and broken, and how amount and date drift across calls), and acoustic signals (speech rate, pause length, pitch variation, which shift with stress). Third, these combine with ledger context — recent UPI settlements, khata balance, payment history — so a borrower who always pays late but always pays is not confused with one whose pattern has broken. Fourth, the agent produces a one-paragraph brief and a distress score, with the exact quotes behind it, so a human can verify it in seconds. Finally, the recommended action comes from a lender-defined playbook: nothing is done, sent, or promised to the borrower without human approval.

Privacy is designed in: audio is processed with the borrower's consent, and the distress score is an internal triage signal, never a credit decision on its own. A borrower flagged as distressed gets a softer, earlier conversation — not a penalty.

4. Why this is new

Three things set it apart from existing collection tooling. First, it listens before the default, not after it: distress can surface in conversation before the ledger shows a miss. Second, it is built for the borrower who exists in this market — Hindi-Hinglish speaking, paid over UPI, keeping a khata — rather than for an English call centre. Third, it is explainable by construction: every flag carries its evidence in the borrower's own words, so a manager can audit it and a disputing borrower can be shown exactly what was heard. Most voice analytics in banking scores the agent; this one listens to the borrower.

5. Feasibility and prototype plan

The Round 2 prototype will use free-tier tools, reusing our existing agent core: Hindi-Hinglish transcription, a small language model for signal extraction, and a rules layer for the lender playbook, delivered via a simple dashboard plus a WhatsApp-style interface. We will test it on simulated collection calls built from realistic scripts — job loss, medical emergency, seasonal slump, and the always-pays-late-but-pays case — measuring whether it flags genuine distress earlier than the ledger alone, without flagging the reliable-but-late borrower. The pilot targets three to five lenders or microfinance field teams, measured on earlier detection, fewer escalations to harsh recovery, and more loans restructured instead of written off.

6. Impact

For a lender, the gain is earlier, cheaper, kinder intervention: a restructured loan keeps a customer and recovers more than a write-off, and a conversation two weeks earlier costs far less than recovery started two months late. For the borrower, the gain is dignity: distress met with a restructured plan instead of pressure. At scale, the same agent can spot a small business whose cash flow is wobbling and offer help before the loan sours. India's credit system already talks to its borrowers every day — it should start listening to them.
